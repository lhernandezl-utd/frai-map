The Teacher Assistant

Two problems, one answer box

The first problem is volume. Each lesson arrives with a teacher guide, a slide deck, a student handout, an activity, an exit ticket, an answer key, discussion scaffolds, a glossary, and video scripts. All of it is needed. None of it is what a teacher wants at 7:50 in the morning, when the real question is "what do I print, and did I need to cut those cards last night?"

The second problem is the one that matters more. These lessons ask teachers to teach how machine learning works, what a weight is, why a language model invents a convincing false sentence, and how bias enters a system that holds no opinions. Many of the teachers delivering them are teaching AI for the first time and were never taught it themselves. The materials tell them what to say. They do not, on their own, make them understand it.

Both problems have the same solution: put the whole curriculum behind a question box, and let it answer either kind of question.

So it does two jobs

① It helps you run the lesson

Everything in the materials, on demand.

Objectives, the run of show at 45 and at 90 minutes, activity steps, exactly what to print and how many, exit tickets and their answer keys with reteach signals, common misconceptions and how to respond to each, differentiation and language supports, vocabulary, and what each video says.

② It helps you understand the subject

This is the part teachers ask for and rarely get.

Ask it what a weight actually is, where the numbers come from, why a chatbot makes things up, or whether a neural network really works like a brain. It explains the concept to you, one layer deeper than the lesson asks scholars to know, and then gives you the classroom version separately.

Why one layer deeper matters. A teacher who understands exactly as much as the scholars can deliver the lesson but cannot survive the unexpected question, and the unexpected question is where these lessons live. The assistant is built so that a teacher always knows more than they need to say.

The design rule that makes it trustworthy

It answers only from the curriculum materials. It has no general knowledge and no web access. When something is not in the materials, it says so plainly instead of guessing, and it will not invent a lesson, an activity, an exit-ticket item, a rubric line, a standard, a file name, or a runtime.

That restraint is the whole point. An assistant that occasionally invents a plausible answer is worse than no assistant at all, because a teacher will act on it in front of a class. This one would rather tell you it does not know.

Real questions, and what comes back. Tell it which grade you teach, because grades 10, 11, and 12 cover the same topics at different depths; if you forget, it will ask before answering rather than guess.

Before class
"I teach grade 10 Lesson 2. What do I need to print, and what do I need to do before class?" The full print list with the exact file names, what to project versus print, and how many of each. Then the prep steps in order, including the one that decides whether the lesson works: cut the card sets ahead of class, keep the two rounds separate, and keep the second round face down.
"Should I tell students that Round 2 is rigged?" No, and it will tell you why in one line. It also gives you the question to ask instead, and tells you not to explain the failure yourself no matter how long the silence runs.
"When do I give the pre-assessment?" Once per unit, before the unit's first lesson, never inside a lesson. It knows which grade has a second one and when.
During class, when the plan meets the room
"I only have 45 minutes and the activity ran long. What do I cut?" It asks your grade first, because the safe cuts are different in each and are not interchangeable. Then it tells you what to drop and what to protect no matter what.
"My class has no devices today. Can I still teach the prompting lesson?" Yes, at full value, and it tells you which pages to print instead. No lesson's 45-minute core depends on a working device. That was a design decision, not luck.
"A student insists a chatbot is general AI. What do I say?" The move that works: ask the scholar to name something the tool cannot do. Narrow AI always has a boundary, and finding the boundary is the proof. It will also flag this as the misconception most likely to show up on the exit ticket.
After class
"Give me the full exit ticket answer key for grade 11 Lesson 1." The complete key, item by item, including the scoring notes that are easy to miss, such as which item is scored on the fit between the label and the reason rather than on the label itself.
"Half my class missed item 4. What now?" The reteach signal attached to that item, what the miss means about what did not land, and whether it needs fixing before the next lesson or can wait.
"Can students answer the exit ticket in Spanish?" Yes, on the open items, in every grade. We score the idea, not the English.
This is the tab worth showing people. Anyone can build a search box over lesson plans. The questions below are the ones a teacher is least likely to ask a colleague out loud, and they are exactly the ones that decide whether a lesson survives contact with a curious class.
The mechanism
"I don't really understand what a weight is. Can you explain it to me?" A number for how much one input matters, anchored to the paper activity your scholars will compute by hand, with the arithmetic worked through.
"Where do the weights actually come from? Who picks the numbers?" Nobody picks them. It walks you through the five-step training loop, ending on the sentence that matters: that loop is what the word "learning" means here, and no understanding is involved at any point.
"What does 'deep' actually mean in deep learning?" It counts layers. Nothing more. Not profound, not wise. This is a real misconception and it is on an exit ticket.
"Should I teach that to my students?" Usually no, and it will say so and tell you what to say instead. It knows the difference between what a teacher should understand and what a lesson should cover.
Why it goes wrong
"Why does it make things up? Is it broken?" No. It is trained to be plausible, not to be true, and nothing inside it checks a claim against the world. A fluent false answer is the design working correctly, which is a far more useful thing for a scholar to understand than "sometimes it makes mistakes."
"Why is it so bad at counting letters or doing arithmetic?" Because it predicts likely text rather than executing a procedure, and counting is a procedure.
"Why do two AI tools give different answers to the same question?" Different training text, different tuning, and deliberate randomness in how each next word is chosen. It will suggest showing this to a class rather than hiding it, because it makes prediction visible.
"How can a computer be biased? It has no opinions." It does not need any. The three doors bias comes through, with the one teachers and scholars both consistently forget.
The questions scholars ask that go past the lesson
"A student is going to ask if it's conscious. What do I say?" The move that redirects to the mechanism the class just ran by hand, plus an honest note that in the older grades this stops being a distraction and becomes the assessed question.
"Does the AI learn from my students while they're chatting with it?" Two separate things, kept separate. What it does inside one conversation, and what may be stored and used later, which depends on the tool and its settings.
"Can I just tell my students it works like a brain? It's easier." Use the analogy, then break it in the same breath, which is what the lessons do on purpose. It will give you the one-line correction and the teacher move that lands it in about fifteen seconds.
"Isn't it just copying what people wrote?" Not in the sense of retrieving and pasting; there is no stored copy to look up. It will also tell you which part of that question is genuinely unsettled rather than pretending it is closed.

What one of these answers looks like

"Where do the weights actually come from? Who picks the numbers?" Abbreviated. The full answer also covers the field's terms for this, and what to do when a scholar asks.

Nobody picks them. Here is the layer underneath what the lesson tells scholars.

  1. The weights start out as essentially random numbers. The system begins knowing nothing.
  2. It makes a prediction on one example whose real answer is already known. Almost certainly a wrong prediction, at the start.
  3. It measures how wrong. The gap between what it said and the right answer is a number.
  4. It nudges every weight a little, in whichever direction would have made that gap smaller.
  5. It repeats this millions of times, across enormous numbers of examples.

Slowly, the weights settle into a configuration that gets the answer right most of the time. That entire loop is what the word "learning" means in machine learning. No understanding is involved at any point. It is repeated adjustment toward less wrongness.

Modern systems have billions of these numbers. There is no person who chose them and no document listing them, which is also why nobody can fully explain why a system produced one specific answer. That is an open research problem, not a gap in your knowledge.

In the classroom, though: stop at "training data sets the weights" and point back to the earlier lesson. If a scholar asks how they get set, celebrate the question, then move on, because the arithmetic on the sheet is what has to land in that period.

Every answer ends by naming the document it came from, so a teacher who wants to check it can find the source in one step.

Two answers that are correct rather than missing. There is no state standards code for the two older grades, because that course is governed by its own international subject guide. And the materials for those two grades are drafts open to your feedback, not final. The assistant will tell you both rather than papering over them.

Who it is for, and how access works

It is for teachers, and it contains the full answer keys, exit-ticket items, and rubrics. That was a deliberate decision: an assistant without the keys cannot answer half of what a teacher actually asks. The consequence is that it is not for scholars, and access has to be handled accordingly.

  • It needs no sign-in and no account, which is what makes it work for every teacher on any device.
  • It embeds anywhere that accepts a standard web embed, so it can live in a teacher-only area of your learning platform, a staff team or channel, a teacher resource page, or a professional development session.
  • It does not go on a page scholars open. A teacher resource page is right; a lesson page scholars open is not. Because there is no sign-in, wherever it is placed is the access control.
  • When you pass it to a teacher, say so out loud: it holds the answer keys, so please do not forward it to scholars.

To arrange access, or to ask for a version aimed at a different audience, contact the university team. Nothing is required on your side to keep it current: the content is maintained centrally and refreshed whenever the lesson materials change, and the embed code stays the same.
