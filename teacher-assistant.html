Grounded in this curriculum, not the open internet.

Ask about any concept in the course, at whatever depth you want it. The answer comes back consistent with how this curriculum teaches it, drawn from the subject-matter expert's material and the built lessons, so what you read matches what your scholars will meet in class.

It answers only from the curriculum materials. No general knowledge, no web search. When something is not in the materials it says so plainly rather than guessing, and it will not invent a lesson, an activity, an exit-ticket item, a rubric line, a standard, or a file name. Real questions, and what comes back. Tell it which grade you teach, because grades 10, 11, and 12 cover the same topics at different depths; if you forget, it will ask before answering rather than guess.

Before class
"I teach grade 10 Lesson 2. What do I need to print, and what do I need to do before class?" The full print list with the exact file names, what to project versus print, and how many of each. Then the prep steps in order, including the one that decides whether the lesson works: cut the card sets ahead of class, keep the two rounds separate, and keep the second round face down.
"Is there anything in the grade 10 Lesson 2 activity I should not reveal to my class?" Yes, and it is the whole lesson. That activity has a deliberate twist that only works if students discover it themselves. The assistant tells you what to withhold, what to say instead, and not to explain the outcome yourself however long the silence runs.
"When do I give the pre-assessment?" Once per unit, before the unit's first lesson, never inside a lesson. It knows which grade has a second one and when.
During class, when the plan meets the room
"I only have 45 minutes and the activity ran long. What do I cut?" It asks your grade first, because the safe cuts are different in each and are not interchangeable. Then it tells you what to drop and what to protect no matter what.
"My class has no devices today. Can I still teach the prompting lesson?" Yes, at full value, and it tells you which pages to print instead. No lesson's 45-minute core depends on a working device. That was a design decision, not luck.
"A student insists a chatbot is general AI. What do I say?" The move that works: ask the scholar to name something the tool cannot do. Narrow AI always has a boundary, and finding the boundary is the proof. It will also flag this as the misconception most likely to show up on the exit ticket.
After class
"Give me the full exit ticket answer key for grade 11 Lesson 1." The complete key, item by item, including the scoring notes that are easy to miss, such as which item is scored on the fit between the label and the reason rather than on the label itself.
"Half my class missed the same exit-ticket item. What should I reteach?" The reteach signal attached to that item, what the miss tells you did not land, and whether it needs fixing before the next lesson or can wait.
"Can students answer the exit ticket in Spanish?" Yes, on the open items, in every grade. We score the idea, not the English.
Ask it about the subject, not just the lesson plan. These are the questions a curious class produces, and the ones that are awkward to go looking for in the middle of a week. Ask them here and get an answer that matches how this curriculum teaches it.
The mechanism
"I don't really understand what a weight is. Can you explain it to me?" A number for how much one input matters, anchored to the paper activity your scholars will compute by hand, with the arithmetic worked through.
"Where do the weights actually come from? Who picks the numbers?" Nobody picks them. It walks you through the five-step training loop, ending on the sentence that matters: that loop is what the word "learning" means here, and no understanding is involved at any point.
"What does 'deep' actually mean in deep learning?" It counts layers. Nothing more. Not profound, not wise. This is a real misconception and it is on an exit ticket.
"Should I teach how models are trained to my students?" No, and it will tell you what to say instead. It keeps a clear line between what is useful for a teacher to understand and what belongs in a 45-minute lesson.
Why it goes wrong
"Why does a chatbot make things up? Is it broken?" No. It is trained to be plausible, not to be true, and nothing inside it checks a claim against the world. A fluent false answer is the design working correctly, which is a far more useful thing for a scholar to understand than "sometimes it makes mistakes."
"Why is a chatbot so bad at counting letters or doing arithmetic?" Because it predicts likely text rather than executing a procedure, and counting is a procedure.
"Why do two AI tools give different answers to the same question?" Different training text, different tuning, and deliberate randomness in how each next word is chosen. It will suggest showing this to a class rather than hiding it, because it makes prediction visible.
"How can a computer be biased? It has no opinions." It does not need any. The three doors bias comes through, with the one teachers and scholars both consistently forget.
The questions scholars ask that go past the lesson
"A student is going to ask whether the AI is conscious. What do I say?" The move that redirects to the mechanism the class just ran by hand, plus an honest note that in the older grades this stops being a distraction and becomes the assessed question.
"Does the AI learn from my students while they're chatting with it?" Two separate things, kept separate. What it does inside one conversation, and what may be stored and used later, which depends on the tool and its settings.
"Can I just tell my students a neural network works like a brain? It's easier." Use the analogy, then break it in the same breath, which is what the lessons do on purpose. It will give you the one-line correction and the teacher move that lands it in about fifteen seconds.
"Is generative AI just copying what people already wrote?" Not in the sense of retrieving and pasting; there is no stored copy to look up. It will also tell you which part of that question is genuinely unsettled rather than pretending it is closed.

What one of these answers looks like

"Where do the weights actually come from? Who picks the numbers?" Abbreviated. The full answer also covers the field's terms for this, and what to do when a scholar asks.

Nobody picks them. Here is the layer underneath what the lesson tells scholars.

  1. The weights start out as essentially random numbers. The system begins knowing nothing.
  2. It makes a prediction on one example whose real answer is already known. Almost certainly a wrong prediction, at the start.
  3. It measures how wrong. The gap between what it said and the right answer is a number.
  4. It nudges every weight a little, in whichever direction would have made that gap smaller.
  5. It repeats this millions of times, across enormous numbers of examples.

Slowly, the weights settle into a configuration that gets the answer right most of the time. That entire loop is what the word "learning" means in machine learning. No understanding is involved at any point. Modern systems have billions of these numbers, and nobody chose them individually.

In the classroom, though: stop at "training data sets the weights" and point back to the earlier lesson. If a scholar asks how they get set, celebrate the question, then move on, because the arithmetic on the sheet is what has to land in that period.

Every answer ends by naming the document it came from, so a teacher who wants to check it can find the source in one step.

Two answers that are correct rather than missing. There is no TEKS code for grades 11 and 12, because Theory of Knowledge is governed by the IB TOK Guide rather than by state standards. And the grade 11 and 12 materials are drafts open to your feedback, not final, while grade 10 is locked. The assistant states both rather than papering over them.

Using it at your campus

  • It needs no sign-in, so it works for every teacher on any device.
  • It embeds anywhere that takes a standard web embed: a teacher area of your learning platform, a staff team or channel, a teacher resource page, or a professional development session.
  • It is a teacher resource, and it holds the answer keys, so keep it to teacher spaces rather than pages scholars open.
  • Nothing is required on your side to keep it current. The content is maintained centrally and refreshed whenever the lesson materials change, and the embed code never changes.
