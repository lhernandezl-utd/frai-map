AI Learning Pathways for Staff

STAFF PROFESSIONAL LEARNING · one shared core, then three pathways PROPOSAL · for discussion, nothing here is decided v0.3 · November 18, 2026 Built from the partner’s own analysis of staff roles and our first outline of the pathways
What we propose to teach staff about AI after the foundations course they already take: a shared core for every role, then practice in each role’s own work and tool. Who it is for, why it is shaped this way, every module and its lessons, and what we would like to decide together.
The idea in one paragraph. Staff already start with the partner’s foundations course on AI literacy and ethics. This page proposes what comes after it. First, one shared core of 7 short modules that every role takes: how to ask well, how to check a draft, what information may go in, and how to judge and respond. No tool is named in the core. Then three pathways, one for each kind of work, where staff practice their own tasks in their own tool, and a few go on to lead. Every module teaches one skill, has the learner practice it on their own work, and ends with a task from their real job. The first core module is built as a working sample, for all three roles. Everything else on this page is a plan.
What we would like from our next conversation. Three answers: (1) whether this shape, a shared core and then three pathways by kind of work, matches how you see your staff, and what you would move; (2) whether “Foundations” in the role analysis is this common level; (3) whether the first three core modules are the right place to start. Tab ⑧ has these and the questions that can wait. Tab ② is the reasoning, and tab ③ is the core, lesson by lesson.

② Why This Shape · what the role analysis told us

What the partner’s role analysis gives us, and what it leaves open. It sorts every staff role into 14 role groups by daily work and by how sensitive their information is. For each group it names the everyday tasks AI could help with (65 in all), a primary tool, how far the training should go, the data rule, and how the group prefers to learn. It does not name the courses: no module, outline or sequence. That part is what this page proposes.
Reason 1

The same skills come first, whatever the job

A teacher, a registrar and a finance analyst use different tools on different tasks, but all three need to ask well, check a draft, and know what information may go in. Taught once, as a shared core, those skills reach every role, and they do not depend on any one tool.

Reason 2

Practical from the first module

The core is not a theory course. The foundations course already gives the ideas. Each core module is one skill, practiced on the learner’s own task, with examples for their role. Tab ③ shows every lesson and what the learner does in it.

Reason 3

Then the work decides the examples

The everyday tasks in the role analysis cluster by where the work happens: a classroom (lesson materials, feedback, family messages), a campus (communication, records, decisions about scholars) and a central office (reports, hiring, budgets, strategy). So role practice splits into three pathways.

Reason 4

It builds on what the partner already has

The foundations course asks each learner about their kind of work: teacher, campus staff or central office. The core and the pathways use that same choice, so staff meet one system. The partner’s AI guidebook also calls for role-specific guidance in its yearly staff training.

Reason 5

Not everyone goes the whole way

The role analysis sets a different finish line for each group, and most groups stop before the top. A shared core means the first modules we build serve every role, not one pathway at a time. How much of the core each group must take is the partner’s call.

A split we considered and did not choose: by tool. Sorting staff by their primary tool and its data rule would make each pathway one tool and one rule, which is simpler to build. We kept the split by kind of work because it is the choice staff already make in the foundations course, and because tools enter only at role practice, through a tool card (tab ⑤).
The cost of our choice, stated plainly. At role practice, each pathway holds more than one tool and more than one data rule, and the Campus pathway holds the most. A module would open by asking the learner’s role and then show only that role’s tool card and data rule. That mechanism is half built: the sample module does it for the examples and the practice task of three roles; a tool card and a data rule by role are designed, not built. Campus is also the least settled pathway: special education, counseling, and college and career advising are specialist work, and tab ④ shows that fewer than half of their tasks have a planned module. Whether Campus should be one pathway or two is a question for the partner.

③ The Core · seven modules for every role, lesson by lesson

What the core is. 7 modules that every role on a pathway takes after the foundations course, about 5¼ hours in all. By the end of the core, any staff member can ask an AI tool for a useful draft, check it before anyone else sees it, decide what information may go in, test a draft for fairness, verify before acting, respond when something goes wrong, and say what they would and would not hand to an agent. Each module has three lessons, one hands-on challenge, a four-question check, and a task to use that week. No tool is named: the practice is done in the approved AI tool the learner already has, on work that needs no personal information. Every on-screen activity uses a fixed set of examples chosen for the learner’s role, so the course can check the answer. Five of the seven hands-on challenges are done in the AI tool; the two about verifying and about responding to an incident are routines a person writes down.

 Core 1 · shared by all three pathways

Ask Well: From a Vague Request to a Useful Draft

By the end, a learner can turn a one-line request into a prompt that returns a usable first draft, steer that draft, and keep the prompt for next time.

LessonThe ideaWhat the learner does on the screen
1 · Start from your taskAn AI tool knows nothing about your task until you say it: who it is for, what you need and in what form. The five parts from the foundations course (context, role, action, format, tone), now on a real task.A sample task for your role, with eight details: sort each into “needed for this task” or “leave it out”, then watch them become a prompt.
2 · Ask for options, then chooseA first answer is a draft, not the answer. Ask for three versions, say what to change, and choose.A scripted practice chat, not your own tool yet: send three follow-ups and pick the best of four options. One of them has a detail that is not true.
3 · Keep what worksA prompt that worked is worth keeping. Saved as a template with blanks, the next request takes a minute.Turn the finished prompt into a template and mark the blanks.

Hands-on challenge, by role (about 15 minutes, on the learner’s own work):

TeachersThree exit ticket questions at different levels for next week’s lesson, steered once
CampusA notice, a checklist or an agenda you owe this week (for example a family notice, a front-office checklist, a meeting agenda), from facts you supply, steered once
CentralA summary, an FAQ or a procedure note for a process you own (for example a payroll calendar note, a help-desk answer, an enrollment FAQ), from facts you supply, steered once

Put it to work, every role: Keep the result as a draft until you have reviewed it. Save the prompt as your first template

Check, four questions: What to leave out · what fixes a general answer · how to steer a draft · what to save as a template

Scholars learn the idea in: Prompt Engineering 101 and 201 (G10 L4 · G11 L5 · G12 L3)

Also: Core 1 opens with a four-minute set-up: using an approved AI tool, signing in with a work account, starting a new chat, and what to do when the tool says no.

 Core 3 · shared by all three pathways

Check It Before You Use It

By the end, a learner can catch what an AI draft gets wrong before anyone else reads it.

LessonThe ideaWhat the learner does on the screen
1 · Why it sounds right and is wrongAn AI tool writes what is likely to come next. The writing itself does not check the facts, so it can invent a date, a quote or a source, and still sound sure.Three confident statements from an AI draft: find the invented one, and say what you would check.
2 · The five-point checkFive questions for any draft: does it fit the purpose, are the facts right, do the sources exist, is it fair, is the language right.An AI draft with five problems: find each one and name the check that catches it.
3 · Open the sourceA summary is only as good as the source behind it. Even when a tool shows links or footnotes, open them: it can cite a page that does not say that. A second AI tool is not a source.A draft makes two claims and cites one short source: open it, and find the claim the source does not support.

Hands-on challenge, by role (about 15 minutes, on the learner’s own work):

TeachersRun the five-point check on the three exit ticket questions you drafted in Core 1, before a scholar sees them
CampusRun the five-point check on the draft you made in Core 1: every date, name and number against its source
CentralRun the five-point check on the summary or note you made in Core 1: open the document and check three claims

Put it to work, by role: Teachers: Use one question in class that week; note in your lesson plan what the check caught · Campus: Use it only after the check; note what it caught · Central: Share it with a note of what you corrected

Check, four questions: Why a fluent draft can be wrong · the five points · what counts as a source · what to do with a claim you cannot confirm

Scholars learn the idea in: When AI Gets It Wrong (G11 L6 · G12 L4) · Using AI Responsibly (G10 L5)  ·  Concept video we already have: Check it before you send it

Agents and Helpers: What to Hand Off, What to Keep

By the end, a learner can tell a tool that answers from a tool that acts, and decide what they would and would not hand to it.

LessonThe ideaWhat the learner does on the screen
1 · Answer, draft, actThe foundations course names three kinds of AI: AI that predicts, AI that produces, and AI that acts by carrying out steps on its own. This lesson is about the third.Sort six everyday tools and tasks by what the AI is doing.
2 · What to hand off, what to keepA routine is a chain of steps. Hand a step to an agent only when it can be undone and a person sees the result before it goes anywhere. The check and the decision stay with a person.One routine in five steps: mark the step an agent could draft and the check a person keeps.
3 · Before you let it actThree questions first: what can it reach, what can it change, and how would you know if it went wrong. Some uses need a review before anyone tries them.Three proposals for an agent: choose fine to try, ask for a review before trying, or not for an agent.

Hands-on challenge, by role (about 15 minutes, on the learner’s own work):

TeachersMap one weekly classroom routine and mark the step you would hand off. Then have your AI tool try that one step, and note what a person still has to check
CampusMap one weekly campus routine and mark the step you would hand off. Then have your AI tool try that one step, and note what a person still has to check

⑤ Tools and Data · the core is tool-neutral, tools come in at role practice

The design rule. The core names no tool: how to ask well, how to check a draft and what information never goes into an AI tool hold whatever the tool is, so the core survives a change of tools and does not wait for the tool list. A tool appears only at role practice, where the hands-on challenge is done in the tool the learner actually has, through a short tool card for their role. If a tool changes, only the card changes.

How a module handles tools and data

  • Tool practice comes from the maker’s own documentation, quoted and linked, so staff learn each tool the way its maker teaches it and the card stays current when the tool changes. Where a maker offers its own training, the tool card points to it and does not repeat it.
  • The partner’s rules are shown as the partner’s rules, cited to the section of the AI guidebook they come from, and kept apart from good practice. A screen never presents good practice as a rule.
  • Every hands-on challenge uses the learner’s own work, with fictional names. The course cannot see the AI tool, and says so; the learner checks their own result against a short list.

Ask Well: From a Vague Request to a Useful Draft

the sample · Core 1 · all three roles · checked on our side

Welcome, goals and role. Three goals to check off, then one question: teacher, campus staff or central office. The answer sets every example and the practice task.

Set-up. Four things before a first prompt: an approved tool, the work account, a new chat for a new task, and what to do when the tool says no.

Lesson 1 · Start from your task. The learner sorts eight details into “needed for this task” or “leave it out”, then sees them as a structured prompt.

Lesson 2 · Ask for options, then choose. A practice chat with a written script: three follow-ups, then four options, one of which has a detail that is not true.

Lesson 3 · Keep what works. For six parts of the prompt, the learner decides “make it a blank” or “keep it as written”, and the template appears.

Hands-on challenge. A prompt builder for the learner’s own task, steps for their own AI tool, and a self-check list.

Check, then before you go. Four questions, three takeaways, the task for that week, and what is planned next.

How it is built

format and tracking

Same format as the foundations course. One package for the partner’s professional learning platform (SCORM 2004, 4th Edition, the standard package format such platforms read), with the same look, navigation and tracking, so staff meet one system.

One role picker, three roles. The same choice the foundations course asks for. A teacher drafts exit ticket questions, a campus office drafts a notice to families, a central office team drafts an internal FAQ.

Length. 24 slides, about 45 minutes, self-paced. A learner can stop and resume, and comes back to the same slide with the same role.

Completion. Complete when every part is done; passed when the check is passed. A learner is never marked failed.

Access. Every activity works with a keyboard and on a phone; nothing is drag only. All names and scenarios are fictional. One small page, with no video.

Where it stands

honest status

Built, and checked by an automated run. The run takes the whole module from start to finish in each of the three roles, at desktop and phone widths, and checks the record it would send to a learning platform. No learner has used it yet.

Reviewed on our side only. Four reviews before anyone at the partner saw it: against the partner’s AI guidebook, by three kinds of learner, for accuracy and teaching design, and for tracking and accessibility. It is not yet tested in the partner’s platform, and it is not in the partner’s hands.

Shown directly, not posted here. The sample is written for the partner by name, so we would show it at the meeting or send it directly. We would like the partner’s comments on it before we build another.

One scenario for each role. A nurse, a counselor or a recruiter picks the nearest of three roles today. More role examples are content, not new code.

An earlier sample exists too. Plan with AI: Practice for Every Scholar was built for teachers before this shape. It holds the teacher version of one lesson of Core 3.

Mostly live, with the online module as the core. In the role analysis, twelve of the fourteen role groups prefer a live format: workshops, team sessions, labs, briefings. So we propose that each module also comes with a one-page facilitator sheet, so a team session, a workshop or a lab can run from it. Who leads those sessions is a question in tab ⑧.

⑧ To Decide Together · for our next conversation

What we propose to build first, in order.
  1. The core, part 1: Ask Well, Keep It Safe, Check It Before You Use It. Every role takes them, no tool is named, and the first one is already built as a working sample.
  2. The core, part 2: the four judgment modules.
  3. Practice in your role, Teachers first. Teachers are the largest group (54.5% of staff).
  4. The awareness step, once we know how those groups are reached.
  5. The two add-on modules, with the partner’s specialists and legal team.
  6. The Lead level last. It serves the fewest people.

Three questions that decide the next step

  1. Does this shape match how you see your staff: a shared core for every role, then three pathways by kind of work? What would you move? Campus is the one we are least sure of: should special education, counseling, and college and career advising stay inside it, or become a pathway of their own?
  2. Is “Foundations” in the role analysis this common level? We read it as the foundations course staff already take, plus the core. If it means the existing course alone, the core becomes the first part of role training. Either way it decides how far student support, operations and front office, and the aides and tutors go.
  3. We propose to start with the first three core modules. Do you agree, and roughly how many people would take them first?

Questions about the role analysis

  1. Is the tool list final? Has the partner’s technology team confirmed it, and are the tools assigned to the groups the list names? Does the partner’s agreement with each tool’s maker include training from the maker? If it does, our modules would point to it for the tool steps and not repeat it.
  2. Who leads the live sessions the role analysis describes, and would a facilitator sheet for each module help them?
  3. Where does special education belong: with teachers or with campus staff? And who at the partner could shape and review the compliance and sensitive data modules?
  4. Is the foundations course meant to reach the awareness groups, on a phone and in two languages? Should any other module exist in a second language?
  5. Which of the open tasks in tab ④ matter most to you? They set what we outline next.

Questions about running the pathways

  1. Who could review the sample module, who approves a module before it goes into the professional learning platform, and who keeps modules current when a tool or the AI guidebook changes?
  2. How much staff time is realistic, and how much of the core should every role take? Part 1 of the core is about 2¼ hours, the whole core about 5¼, and a whole pathway with the core about 10¾ to 11½. We would rather cut than have staff not finish.
  3. Who reviews a pathway challenge? The outline says a campus AI lead or coach; that is the partner’s choice.
  4. Where does video live: inside each package, or on a platform the partner hosts?
  5. Do the pathways count as professional development hours, as the yearly AI training the guidebook describes, or both? Does a badge mark each level?
  6. What evidence of impact does the partner want: completion, check results, a short staff survey?

What we changed after reading the role analysis

  • A shared core. Our first outline put prompting, checking and data safety inside single pathways. They are now one core that every role takes first.
  • Tool cards. Our first outline named no tool. At role practice, the hands-on challenge would become specific to each role group’s tool, once the partner confirms the list.
  • A finish line for each role group, instead of everyone taking a whole pathway.
  • Two add-on modules (special education compliance; sensitive data for student support) and one awareness step.
  • A facilitator sheet for each module, because most role groups prefer to learn live.
  • A list of open tasks (tab ④) that our outline does not cover yet, most of them in the Campus and Central pathways.
