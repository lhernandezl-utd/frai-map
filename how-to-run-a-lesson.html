How to Run a Lesson, Start to Finish

A WORKED EXAMPLE · Lesson 1 in all three grades SUGGESTED, not required v1.2 · July 28, 2026

The course maps show what is taught and how the pieces are built. This page shows what a teacher actually does with them: before the lesson, minute by minute inside it, and after. Three complete lessons are worked through, Lesson 1 in each grade, plus a table matching every element you are given to the moment you use it.

This is a suggestion, not a script. It is one worked example of how the pieces fit together, offered so the materials are easy to pick up. It is a recommended model and not a mandate: how a lesson is delivered, and where it sits in the calendar, is the school's decision, campus by campus. Every lesson in every grade is built to this same shape, so once one lesson makes sense, they all do.

Where to start · the first three things you do with any lesson

Step 1
Open the Lesson Plan

One document per lesson. Read Lesson at a glance first: the question, the outcome, and the one idea to protect. Three minutes.

Step 2
Print from the prep table

It is at the top of that same plan. Exact file names, exact copy counts, and anything to cut ahead. You never decide what to print.

Step 3
Open the deck, press play once

The finished videos are already inside the slide deck at the point where each one plays. Nothing to download or queue.

The time model · 45 minutes is the standard

Every lesson is designed to be complete in one 45-minute period. The full content is taught, the objective is met, and it is checked, inside those 45 minutes. That is the design, not a compressed version of something longer. What changes is what a school does with any time beyond it.

Your scheduleThe lesson itselfAny time beyond it
45-minute periodGrade 10 Complete at 45. Objective taught, met, and checked. Nothing is left undone. None needed. The lesson is finished.
90-minute blockGrade 10 The same complete 45. Every lesson carries a second 45 that is already written. You choose: run the next lesson back to back to keep pace, or stay on this lesson and use its built-in extension to go deeper. The second 45 is never required to finish the lesson.
Theory of KnowledgeGrades 11 and 12 Complete at 45, in the course's own gradual-release shape. There is no second period, and none is assumed. TOK does not get a block, so the depth was built inside the lesson instead: the going-deeper debate, about 8 to 10 minutes, plus an optional extension offered after class.
The one thing to get right in week one: the pre-assessment sits outside the 45 It is given before that unit's first lesson and takes about 15 minutes. It is never part of the lesson, because folding it in would push teaching out of the period.
  • On a single 45-minute period, and in all of TOK: give it on a separate day beforehand, as its own short stand-alone session. 15 plus 45 does not fit in one period.
  • On a 90-minute block (grade 10 only): give it in the first 15 minutes, then teach the complete 45-minute lesson the same day. That still leaves about half an hour for the extension.

How many you have differs by grade. Your teacher index states your own case in full.

  • Grade 10: one before Lesson 1. Nothing before Lessons 2 to 5.
  • Grade 11: two, because Lesson 5 opens Unit 2. One before Lesson 1 and one before Lesson 5.
  • Grade 12: one course-covering baseline before Lesson 1, because the condensed pass crosses three units in five lessons.

It is your diagnostic, so you know what this class already believes before you teach them. It is never graded.

Grade 10 · Project-Based Capstone · Unit 1 · Lesson 1 · "What Is AI, Really?"

Essential question: What separates true intelligence from automation?  ·  They can now: define AI in their own words and classify everyday technologies as automation, narrow AI, or general AI, justifying each.  ·  The one idea to protect: almost all AI today is narrow, and AI is not one single thing. The goal is to classify it.

Stage 1 Before you teach

  1. Read the Lesson Plan. U1L1_Lesson-Plan_Grade-10.docx

    Start at Lesson at a glance, then the run of show. Everything else in the folder is named from inside this document at the moment you need it.

  2. Print and cut.

    Scholar handout, one per scholar. Sort cards, one set per pair, cut ahead of class. Exit ticket, one per scholar. Teacher copies only: answer key and rubric, discussion scaffolds.

  3. Watch the teacher video once. About two and a half minutes.

    It is the guide's short version: the one idea, the moves to lean on, the misconception to watch. Never shown in class.

  4. Check the pre-assessment is already done.

    Given once, before this lesson, on a prior day or at the start of the block. Not inside the 45 minutes.

Stage 3 If you have a 90-minute block, choose one

Route A · two lessons back to back

Teach Lesson 2 next, as its own complete 45-minute lesson, to keep pace through the unit. Nothing from this lesson is left undone.

Route B · this lesson, deeper

45 to 52 recap and share exit-ticket answers, clear the borderline cases. 52 to 72 the full sort and class chart, justify two, debate the borderline cards. 72 to 85 real-world connection, plus the scenario video and the going-deeper segment. 85 to 90 closing reflection: one thing that changed in how you see AI.

The going-deeper story, for Route B Capability grew but narrowness stayed: a chess AI that beats every human alive still cannot play checkers. Then the case that lands it: a top image classifier called a husky a "wolf", because it had learned to spot the snow in the background, not the animal. Tell it as a story first and name the concept second. It also previews Lesson 2, where the data teaches, for better or worse.

Stage 4 After the lesson

  1. Score the exit tickets against the key.

    The rubric says what a one-point and a two-point response look like, and which misses signal which reteach.

  2. Offer the extension, do not assign it. "AI in My Field."

    One real AI use in the pathway they care about, its category, and one limit, in three sentences. Say it as an invitation: "If today made you curious, here is where to point that." Strong answers become capstone problem candidates.

  3. Hand forward to Lesson 2.

    Close on the question the next lesson answers: "If it is not following typed-in rules, then how does it learn?" That is Lesson 2, and today's sort is what set it up.

Grade 11 · Theory of Knowledge, Year 1 · Unit 1 · Lesson 1 · "What Is AI, Really?"

Knowledge question: How certain can we be about where intelligence ends and automation begins?  ·  They can now: apply three boundary criteria, rules, learning, and transfer, to judge whether a technology counts as AI, and rate how certain that judgment is.  ·  Tags: Knowledge Framework Scope · TOK Concept Certainty.
The one idea to protect: you cannot judge whether something is intelligent until you say what test you are using, and once you say it, you often find the test does not settle the case.
The el. # tags in the right-hand column point to the 18 elements every lesson is built from. Tab ③ explains each one.

Stage 1 Before you teach

  1. Read the Lesson Plan. U1L1-G11_Lesson-Plan_TOK.docx

    It is written in your TOK lesson-plan format, so the fields are the ones you already use. A PDF sits beside it if you prefer reading on a phone.

  2. Print.

    Scholar handout, one per scholar. Exit ticket, one per scholar. Teacher copies only: the in-class activity, which holds the case rationale, plus the answer key and rubric and the discussion scaffolds.

  3. Know the three criteria cold. Rules · Learning · Transfer.

    They are the whole lesson. Rules: fixed steps, the same every time. Learning: behaviour that changes from data. Transfer: carrying what it learned to a new task.

  4. Check the pre-assessment is already done.

    TOK has no block, so it is given in a class period before this lesson, on a separate day.

The lesson ends on the question, not on an answer This is the design, not an unfinished lesson. A grade 10 lesson closes its question and hands scholars a rule. A TOK lesson leaves the question open, in writing, in the scholar's own hand. That open question is the product.

Stage 3 Going deeper · about 8 to 10 minutes, wherever it fits

TOK gets no second block, so the depth lives here rather than in a second period. Note that the 45 minutes above is already full, so this does not sit inside it. The lesson is complete without it; run the debate when you have the room, and place it where it works for your class.

The debate question Is "artificial intelligence" a real category, or just a name for whatever computers cannot do yet?
  1. Position first, silently. One sentence of reason, before any discussion and before any tool.
  2. Two corners, and the middle must be defended too. Undecided is a position, not an escape.
  3. One exchange each way, and the other side must restate the argument accurately before replying.
  4. The switch test. "What evidence would move you?"
  5. Land it honestly. Do not resolve it. Name it as an open question the class just demonstrated.

Three facts that make the debate real: capability grew but narrowness stayed, and a chess AI that beats every human alive cannot play checkers · the image classifier that called a husky a "wolf" because it had learned to spot the snow, which is Turing and Searle made concrete · the name "artificial intelligence" was chosen in the 1950s for a funding proposal, and it keeps moving, since reading text and filtering spam were once flagship AI problems and are now ordinary features.

Stage 4 After the lesson

  1. Score against the key.

    An exemplar response sits in the Teacher Guide, so you can see what full credit reads like before you mark.

  2. Offer the extension, never assign it. "Find the Open Case."

    Find one real technology you genuinely cannot classify with certainty, and defend the fact that you cannot. Grade 10 rewards a clean classification; here the reward is an honest failure to reach one. It feeds the Exhibition.

  3. Hold the academic-integrity line, every lesson.

    AI is the object of study, not a writing aid. Think first, disclose and cite on the handout's AI-use line, check what it returns against the three criteria, and never in an IB submission.

Grade 12 · Theory of Knowledge, Year 2 · Unit 1 · Lesson 1 · "What Is AI and How Machines Learn"

Read this before assuming the grade 11 walkthrough applies Grade 12 uses the same shell as grade 11: Theory of Knowledge, one complete 45-minute period, no block, gradual release, ending on the knowledge question. Everything inside that shell is different. The instrument, the vocabulary of the verdict, the cases, and even the block timings are not the same. Running grade 11's three criteria and its settled / leaning / open scale in a grade 12 room means running the wrong tool.

Knowledge question: If a machine's "knowledge" is patterns in data, what kind of knowing is that?  ·  They can now: judge what we can responsibly claim about an AI system's output once we know how it was trained, tracing data to pattern to prediction as the evidence.  ·  Tags: Knowledge Framework Methods and Tools · TOK Concept Evidence.
The one idea to protect: a trained system's output is a prediction from patterns in its training data, so a claim about that output can carry only as far as its training story.
It condenses two grade 10 lessons into one, "What Is AI" and "How Computers Think", because the senior pass covers the whole arc in eight lessons. The ground covered per minute is greater than in grade 11.

Stage 2 The 45 minutes · gradual release

BlockWhat you doWhat you reach for
5 minDo Now One product claim on the board: "Your music app knows your taste better than your friends do." Scholars vote in writing: fair claim, too strong, or false, and defend the vote in one sentence. No discussion, no resolution. The room now holds three positions about one sentence, and nobody has yet said what the app was trained on. That gap is the lesson. Slide deck el. 5
15 minI do Teach the floor plainly from slides 3 to 5. Slide 4 holds the finished Concept Video 1 (1 min 28 s); play it, let its closing question sit, then continue. Trained, not told: a microwave timer follows fixed rules the same way every time; a spam filter was trained on millions of labelled emails and learned its own patterns. Then the chain: labelled examples (data) → the model finds regularities nobody hand-picked (pattern) → the output is the most likely answer given those regularities (prediction). Then name the claim test: warranted (the training story carries the claim; cite the chain) · overreaching (the claim goes beyond the chain; state the rewrite) · unsupported (the training story speaks against the claim, or never reaches it). Model the whole skill aloud twice: on the Do Now claim itself, which traces to listening histories and lands on overreaching, with a rewrite that keeps the warranted core; and on a chess champion system, where "this machine outthinks humans" is unsupported, because its patterns live entirely inside one game and it cannot play checkers. Step 2, judge: for two trained tools, trace the chain and judge the claim, citing the chain as evidence. Stage 3 After the lesson

  1. Score against the key.

    An exemplar response sits in the Teacher Guide, so you can see what full credit reads like before you mark.

  2. Offer the extension, never assign it.

    Same principle as the other grades: an invitation, not homework.

  3. Hold the academic-integrity line, and hardest of all here.

    The senior year is the Essay year, so this is the grade where the guardrail carries the most weight. AI is a disclosed, cited research aid, and it never generates submission text.

Folder codes are not sequential by unit in grade 12 The condensed pass crosses three units in five delivered lessons, so the folder codes read U1L1, U1L2, U2L3, U2L4, U3L5. A teacher looking for "Lesson 3" wants the folder coded U2L3, because that lesson belongs to Unit 2. This is also why grade 12 has one course-covering baseline instead of one per unit.
